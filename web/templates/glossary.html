Glossary

Plain-language definitions for every term used on this site. If you see a term in a chart or table that isn't here, let me know and I'll add it.

Returns and growth

CAGR — Compound Annual Growth Rate

The constant annual return that, if compounded over the test period, would produce the observed final equity. Formula: (end / start)^(1/years) − 1. Does not account for risk. A strategy with 30% CAGR and 70% drawdown is much worse than one with 20% CAGR and 15% drawdown. Always look at CAGR alongside max drawdown and Sharpe.

Total return

End equity divided by start equity, minus 1. If you started with $100k and ended with $250k, total return is +150%. Equivalent to CAGR if the time period is exactly 1 year.

Win rate

Fraction of closed trades with a positive return. Higher is better, but a 95% win rate strategy where the 5% losers are -100% can still lose money. Always check profit factor too.

Profit factor

Sum of winning trade returns divided by absolute sum of losing trade returns. >1 means winners outweigh losers in absolute return terms. Above 2 is strong; below 1 means the strategy lost money.

Risk

Maximum drawdown (max DD)

The worst peak-to-trough loss the strategy ever experienced, as a percentage of equity at the peak. A −40% max drawdown means at some point the account lost 40% from its prior high before recovering. This is what makes traders panic-sell and abandon a strategy. Smaller drawdowns are far more psychologically tradeable than the same CAGR with a bigger drawdown.

Sharpe ratio

Mean daily return divided by standard deviation of daily returns, multiplied by √252 to annualize. Roughly: how many "units of risk" per "unit of reward." Above 1 = decent; above 2 = strong; above 3 = suspicious (recheck for leakage or survivorship bias).

Daily Sharpe vs annualized Sharpe

If you see "Sharpe ≈ 2.18" without a unit, it's almost certainly annualized. A literal daily Sharpe of 2.18 would imply annualized 2.18·√252 ≈ 34.6, which is impossible for any real strategy. The original document's "daily Sharpe ~2.18" claim is annualized, not daily.

Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR)

If you try N model configurations and report the best, the Sharpe of that best config is biased upward — even if all N have zero true skill. The DSR (Bailey & López de Prado, 2014) deflates the threshold a Sharpe needs to cross to be considered evidence of real skill, by an amount proportional to √(2·log N). A "DSR significance" of 0.95+ means: even after accounting for selection bias from N trials, this Sharpe is unlikely under the null. The original document tried 72+ configs and reported the best — DSR pushes its threshold up substantially.

Pipeline-specific

Anchor date

A date on which the model makes a prediction for a stock. Anchors are weekly (last trading day per ISO calendar week per ticker). For each anchor, the model looks at the prior 252 trading days of price/volume to predict the forward H-day return.

Horizon (H)

How many trading days forward the label measures. H=40 means: did Close[anchor + 40 trading days] exceed Close[anchor] × (1 + threshold)?

Threshold (T)

The required forward return for the label to be "1" (positive). T=0.25 means a 25% forward gain counts as positive, anything less is "0".

Walk-forward validation

Train on years t1…tN, test on year tN+1. Then retrain on years t1…tN+1, test on tN+2. And so on. Stocks change behavior over time, so this is more honest than a random train/test split (which would leak future regimes into the training set).

Purged walk-forward + embargo (López de Prado)

If your label is forward-looking (e.g., 40-day return), then the late-Dec-2018 anchors in the training set have labels resolved using prices from Feb 2019 — which is in the test fold. That's label leakage. The fix is to purge: drop training rows whose label resolution date falls inside the test fold (plus an extra embargo buffer). The original document's walk-forward had no purging — that's why the corrected version implements it.

Prefilter (Close > 1.5 × MA250)

Before running any model, the pipeline restricts attention to stocks whose closing price is at least 1.5× their 250-day moving average. This is a strong "uptrend" filter. Both the model AND the random/baseline portfolios sample from the same prefiltered universe — so the random baseline already inherits significant momentum exposure, which the model then has to beat.

DINOv2

A self-supervised vision transformer trained by Meta AI on natural images. We freeze it and use it to convert 224×224 chart images into 384-dimensional embeddings. Honestly: line charts are wildly out-of-distribution for DINOv2 — the engineered-features baseline (no images) is the more important track to watch.

Slippage · commission · ADV

Slippage: the difference between the price you wanted to fill at and the price you actually got. Buying tends to push the price up against you; selling tends to push it down. Modeled here as 10 bps each side (configurable). Commission: broker fee, here $0.005/share capped at 0.5% of trade value. ADV: average daily dollar volume traded. The simulator caps each position at 0.5% of the stock's 20-day ADV — so you can't buy more than the market will absorb without moving the price.

Statistics

Multiple-comparison bias

If you try 100 strategies and report the one that worked best, you've engineered an upward bias even if all 100 have zero true edge. Standard corrections include Bonferroni (multiply p-value by N), Šidák, BH-FDR, and the Deflated Sharpe Ratio. The original document tried 72+ configs without correcting.

Block bootstrap

A non-parametric way to estimate uncertainty in a time series with autocorrelation (like a daily return series where today's return is correlated with yesterday's). Resample the series in blocks of length B (here B = horizon = 40), refit, repeat 10,000 times, and look at the distribution of the result. This gives honest p-values without assuming returns are independent.

Random baseline

Run the same simulator with the same universe and same rules, but pick stocks at random instead of using the model's score. Run 100–1000 random seeds. If the model's CAGR is below the random baseline's median, the model is worse than picking stocks at random. If it's above the 95th percentile, that's necessary but not sufficient for real edge (see "multiple-comparison bias").
