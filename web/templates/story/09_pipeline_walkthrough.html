The Pipeline, Stage by Stage

All the chapters so far have shown aggregate results. This one picks a single real trade — BioNTech on 2021-07-02 → 2021-08-30, +53.7% — and walks through every stage of the pipeline that touched it. Real data, real features, the actual chart image our AI saw, and the actual outcome.

Stage 1 — the input

For every Friday in our backtest, the pipeline looks at every US stock's prior 252 trading days (about one calendar year) of closing prices and volumes. For BNTX on Friday 2021-07-02, that window covers the year from 2020-07-06 to 2021-07-02 — the post-COVID-vaccine rally.

Stage 2 — the prefilter funnel

Out of all ~6,700 US common stocks in our universe, only the ones with Close > 1.5 × 250-day MA are eligible. This is the pipeline's "momentum prefilter." On 2021-07-02, just 373 stocks passed. Then the simulator applies an ADV filter (≥ $5M daily dollar volume) and a no-duplicate-ticker rule. After those, the simulator buys the top 5 by score.

Stage 3 — engineered features

For each eligible stock we compute 40 features that summarize its recent behavior: returns at multiple time horizons, volatility, drawdowns, distance from moving averages, slope, swing count, volume z-score, and more. These are the inputs to the LightGBM model. Note: this is the engineered-features track that won the falsification test in Chapter 5; the DINOv2 image track sees a different feature representation.

How features feed the model

LightGBM (a gradient-boosted-trees model) is shown these 40 numbers per (ticker, anchor) example, plus the binary label "did the stock go up ≥25% in the next 40 trading days?". It learns combinations of features that historically predicted the label. Critically: it's only trained on data from BEFORE the test fold (see the audit on walk-forward leakage).

Stage 4 — chart image rendering (DINOv2 track)

In parallel with the engineered-features track, the pipeline renders the same 252-day price window as a deterministic 224×224 grayscale chart image and feeds it through Meta's DINOv2 ViT-S/14 image encoder (a 22-million-parameter vision model). The output is a 384-dimensional embedding that summarizes the chart's shape.

Why this stage exists at all

The hypothesis was that DINOv2's image features would catch "chart patterns" that hand-built engineered features miss (cup-and-handle, ascending triangles, etc.). The falsification test found that this didn't actually help — the engineered-features track beat the image track after realistic costs. We keep the image rendering here for completeness; it's the same image our backtest's DINOv2 stack processed.

Stage 4b — what we actually do with the 384 numbers (and why we essentially throw them away)

This is the deep-dive a lot of readers ask for. Once DINOv2 spits out a 384-dim vector for BNTX, where does it go and how much of it survives by the time we get to a probability?

The actual 384 numbers for BNTX on 2021-07-02

Here's BNTX's real DINOv2 embedding, reshaped to a 16×24 grid for visualization. Each cell is one of the 384 floating-point activations the encoder produced. Mean ≈ 0, std ≈ 2.5, range roughly [−7, +8]. There's no human-interpretable meaning to any individual dimension; the model just learned them.

Step 1 — compress 384 numbers down to 64 via PCA

384 dimensions is too many for our downstream linear model on ~58k training samples. So inside each walk-forward fold, the pipeline:

  1. Z-scores the 384 dimensions on the training fold (StandardScaler — mean=0, std=1 per dim)
  2. Fits PCA to 64 components on the training fold
  3. Applies the same fitted scaler + PCA to the test fold (no peeking at test data when fitting — this is the leakage protection)

Two important details about that PCA step:

  • It's unsupervised. PCA finds directions of maximum variance, not directions of maximum predictive signal. Those may or may not be the same thing.
  • It's per-fold. Each test year gets its own PCA fitted from its own training data. The PCA basis changes from fold to fold, so the 64-dim "image features" aren't directly comparable across years.

BNTX's 384 numbers, after PCA: 64 values

Step 2 — combine with 64 volume PCA features

In parallel with the image track, the pipeline takes the past 252 trading days of volume, applies zscore(log1p(volume)), then runs the SAME StandardScaler-then-PCA-64 routine on it. So we end up with another 64-dim vector.

The two are concatenated: 64 (image) + 64 (volume) = 128 features. Then ONE more StandardScaler is applied to the concatenation (to put both halves on a common scale).

Step 3 — feed 128 features into logistic regression → 1 probability

The final stage is shockingly simple given everything that came before. A LogisticRegression with C=1, class_weight='balanced' (because positives are only 5–15% of training anchors), and L2 regularization collapses the 128 features into ONE number: the predicted probability that ret_40d_ge_25pct = 1.

An isotonic calibration step on a held-out 10% slice of the training fold maps the raw probability to a calibrated probability comparable across folds.

The total information funnel

And there's a parallel "engineered" track that does NONE of this

So why is DINOv2 kept in the pipeline at all?

Three reasons we still compute the DINOv2 track even though it loses

  1. To prove it loses. The falsification test only works if we run the AI side-by-side with the non-AI side. Removing it would mean we couldn't show the comparison.
  2. To match the original document. The whole point of this project was to reproduce a specific paper's pipeline honestly. We need to faithfully reproduce what they did to assess what they claimed.
  3. For future research. The 91.5% variance retained by PCA-64 isn't necessarily the same as the predictive signal retained. A finer encoder (1D PatchTST, custom CNN trained from scratch, or per-fold supervised dimensionality reduction) could plausibly extract real signal that PCA throws away. See Research Problem 2 for the proposed bake-off.

What about the volume track? Same treatment.

Volume gets the exact same StandardScaler → PCA-64 compression as the image embeddings do, just starting from a 252-dim vector instead of 384. Same 91%-of-variance trade-off: most variance is kept, all individual-day-volume detail is lost. But here it matters less because the engineered track also computes volume statistics (log_dollar_vol_z252, vol_ratio_20_252, vol_trend_slope_60d) directly from the 252-dim vector, so we have both a compressed view and a hand-built view of volume.

Why class_weight='balanced'

The training labels have ~5–15% positive rate (only ~1 in 10 candidates rises 25% in 40 days). Without class weighting, a logistic regression maximizing log-loss would learn "always predict 0" — which has 90% accuracy but zero discriminative power. Setting class_weight='balanced' reweights the loss so positives and negatives count equally, forcing the model to actually learn the boundary.

The downside, as the falsification chapter notes, is that the output probabilities aren't calibrated to the true rate anymore — they're calibrated to a 50-50 prior. That's fine for RANKING (top-5 per week) but breaks any absolute "buy only if P > 0.6" rule. The isotonic calibration step partially fixes this but doesn't fully reverse the class-weight distortion.

For each of the 373 eligible stocks on 2021-07-02, LightGBM outputs a probability between 0 and 1: "how likely is this stock to be up ≥25% in the next 40 trading days?" Higher = more bullish.

Notice what just happened

The ADV filter is doing real work. Of the 7 highest-scoring stocks, 4 were knocked out as too illiquid to actually trade. The liquidity filter is protecting us from the exact kind of micro-caps the model latches onto.

Stage 7 — what actually happened to BNTX

Entry on 2021-07-02 at $221.07. Exit exactly 40 trading days later, on 2021-08-30, at $340.49. A +54% return in 8 weeks. The model didn't predict this specifically — BNTX wasn't even its top-9 pick — but the simulator's mechanics ended up buying it as part of a diversified cohort.

The lesson

What this one trade tells us about the strategy

The model is noisy: its top-1 confidence pick that week (DBI at probability 1.00) was a loser. The simulator's execution mechanics (ADV filter, no-duplicate rule, position-size cap) do far more work than the raw ranking does. And the actual winners are right-tail surprises — BNTX was a rank-10 candidate that happened to ride a news catalyst (delta variant).

This explains the statistics chapter's result: across thousands of weeks, the cohort means are dominated by a few outliers, not by reliable ranking. Effective sample size is much smaller than naive trade count. The Sharpe is real but not significant after deflation.

import pandas as pd

# Find the biggest winners we actually bought

"
