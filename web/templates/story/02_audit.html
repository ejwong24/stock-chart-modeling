The Audit

Before we touched a line of code, we sent the original document to 12 parallel AI subagents, each instructed to look for a different kind of systematic flaw. They came back with 7 high-severity issues.

The 12 angles of attack

Each subagent took one of these:

  1. Survivorship bias — "Is the universe missing companies that went bankrupt?"
  2. Walk-forward leakage — "Do training labels secretly peek at the future?"
  3. Multiple-comparison bias — "How many configurations did they try before reporting the best?"
  4. Chart auto-scaling — "Does the way they draw the chart destroy information about returns?"
  5. Statistical significance — "Is the apparent edge inside the noise band?"
  6. Encoder choice — "Is DINOv2 a sensible model for line charts?"
  7. Volume features + transaction costs — "What does this cost to actually trade?"
  8. Class imbalance and ranking — "Is the binary 'up 25%' label the right thing to predict?"
  9. Reproducibility — "Could a coworker re-run this and get the same numbers?"
  10. Implementation plan — "How would a clean rebuild be structured?"
  11. Data sourcing — "Where would clean stock data come from?"
  12. Beat-the-baselines — "Did they compare against simple non-AI strategies?"

Each agent wrote a 300-500 word critique with specific recommendations. Then we synthesized them.

The 7 high-severity flaws (and the magnitude each can produce)

#FlawWhat it does to the headlineSeverity
1 Survivorship bias Companies that went bankrupt 2016–2026 are missing from the dataset. The strategy buys exactly the high-momentum small-caps that often crash. With the catastrophes removed, the strategy looks magical. HIGH (+3-10pp CAGR)
2 Walk-forward leakage Training labels for late-Dec anchors resolve in February — which is the test period. The model is implicitly trained on future test-set prices. HIGH
3 Multiple-comparison bias The paper tested 72 different config combinations and reported the one that worked best. Under the null hypothesis (zero true skill) the probability of at least one beating the random 95th percentile is 97.5%. HIGH
4 Chart auto-scaling The chart image auto-fit each stock onto the same canvas, destroying information about whether the stock went up 5% or 500%. The model literally can't see the very thing the label depends on. HIGH (silent)
5 No simple-baseline comparison The paper only compared the model to random picks. But within an already-momentum-prefiltered universe, "random" inherits momentum exposure. A simple 12-month-momentum sort would likely match or beat the AI. HIGH
6 No transaction costs Real trading has slippage, spreads, commissions, halts, taxes. The strategy buys microcaps, where these costs are 60-200 bps round-trip. With 6+ turns/year that's 4-12% per year of pure cost. HIGH
7 DINOv2 is out-of-distribution DINOv2 was trained on photos of cats, dogs, faces. Line charts on white backgrounds are 99% empty pixels. The encoder probably isn't doing what the paper assumed it was doing. MED-HIGH

How big could these flaws be, combined?

Individually, each one might shave 2-5 percentage points off the headline. Combined, they're enough to fully explain the gap between the claim (+61%) and what's actually reproducible (+5 to +17%). The audit predicted this before we ran a single line of code.

What about the 60 deeper subagents?

After the 12-agent audit, we ran a second pass: 5 hard open problems × 12 subagents each = 60 deeper analyses. Each problem (survivorship-bias closure, encoder bake-off, lockbox protocol, transaction-cost calibration, path-dependent exits) got 12 specialized perspectives, then a synthesis chapter.

The audit recommendations drove every correction we made. The next chapter shows what happened when we applied them.
