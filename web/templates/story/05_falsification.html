The Falsification Test

The original paper had two pillars: rendered chart images and a frozen DINOv2 image encoder. We A/B-tested them by running the same pipeline two ways: once with the AI image stack, once with a 30-line non-AI baseline. Then we compared.

Why this is the most important test in the project

If DINOv2-on-charts is doing something real, then a strategy with the image features should beat a strategy without them. If it's not doing anything real, the two should be statistically tied. There's no third option.

This is called the falsification test: a single experiment designed to potentially kill the whole hypothesis. If the AI loses to the non-AI, the AI is decorative. If it wins by a wide margin, we should keep using it. If it barely ties, we should kill it on Occam's-razor grounds.

What each track does

TrackHow it worksLines of code
lgbm_image Render each stock's prior year of prices as a 224×224 line drawing. Feed each image through DINOv2 ViT-S/14 (22M-parameter image AI from Meta). Get 384-dim embedding. Add 64-dim volume features. PCA to 128 dims. Feed to LightGBM. ~600 lines
lgbm_engineered Compute 40 hand-built features per stock: returns at 1d/5d/21d/63d/126d/252d, volatility, drawdowns, MA distances, slope, R², swing count, BB width, return-volume correlation, etc. Feed to LightGBM. No images. No DINOv2. No PCA. ~250 lines

Both use the same labels, same splits, same train/test years, same LightGBM hyperparameters, same simulator, same costs. The only difference is whether the features are AI image embeddings or hand-built numbers.

The answer

DINOv2 + LightGBM (no costs)
+17.27%
Engineered + LightGBM (no costs)
+12.97%
DINOv2 + LightGBM (realistic costs)
+7.13%
Engineered + LightGBM (realistic costs)
+9.79%

The verdict

Without costs: DINOv2 wins by ~4pp CAGR, but with worse drawdowns (−77% vs −70%) and the same Sharpe. Net of risk, it's a tie.

With realistic costs: the engineered baseline wins by 2.6pp CAGR. The image AI was doing something worse than 40 numbers from a 1990s textbook.

This is the falsification firing. The DINOv2 image pipeline is decorative.

Why the AI did worse than expected

The audit predicted this. DINOv2 was trained by Meta on a corpus of ~142 million natural images (photos of cats, dogs, faces, scenes). When you feed it a black line on a white background, ~99% of the pixels are empty. The "embedding" it produces is dominated by image-level features like line density and anti-aliasing artifacts, not by the geometric properties of the price path.

Meanwhile the 40 engineered features were hand-designed by humans who have looked at lots of stock charts. They explicitly encode the things that matter: slope, drawdown depth, days since recent peak, relative-to-moving-average distance, vol of vol, and so on. They're "dumber" than the AI but they're in domain.

The general lesson

When you apply a state-of-the-art AI to a domain it wasn't trained on (line charts ≠ photos), you're betting that generic features will transfer. Sometimes they do. Often they don't. The way to find out is the falsification test: train the simplest possible baseline alongside the fancy one and compare honestly. The original paper never ran this comparison.

So what was creating the original 61% CAGR claim?

Now we can finally answer the question from chapter 1. The original number was a combination of:

  • Survivorship bias in the universe (+3 to +10pp CAGR fake)
  • Walk-forward leakage at fold boundaries (~+1-2pp fake)
  • Best of 72 configs reported as the model (~+5 to +15pp fake under null)
  • No transaction costs assumed (+5 to +10pp removed by reality)
  • "Beat random in a momentum-prefiltered universe" framing (low bar; the random was already strong-momentum)
  • No simple-baseline comparison — if they'd run engineered+LightGBM alongside the AI, they'd have seen the AI wasn't actually helping

Take all six off and the +61% claim collapses to something in the +5% to +10% range pre-cost, which is roughly where the random median sits.
